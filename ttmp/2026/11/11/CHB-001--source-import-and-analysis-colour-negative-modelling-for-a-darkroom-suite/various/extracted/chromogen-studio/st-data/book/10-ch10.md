# Inside the Studio

This chapter describes how the Studio computes a print. Two ideas run through it:

- **Tables.** The expensive physics is evaluated ahead of time into lookup tables.
- **Coordinates.** Almost every control is a cheap change to the coordinates at which those tables are read.

## The per-pixel chain

Every pixel of the view passes through one chain, run as WebGL2 fragment shaders on the GPU:

1. **Linear transmittance $T$** from the file, divided by the flat field if there is one. The Source view's Encoding setting says how to read it: linear (the default for 16-bit files and the sample), sRGB-encoded (the default for 8-bit files) or gamma 2.2.
2. **Scanner density** $D_k = -\log_{10}(T_k / T_k^{\text{rebate}})$.
3. **The analytical estimate** $\mathbf a = K^{-1}(\mathbf D - \mathbf D_{\text{base}})$, where $K$ is the 3×3 matrix of each unit dye's densities in each scanner channel (§9.2).
4. **Dye amounts $\mathbf c$** from the solve table, read at $\mathbf a$; the table also gives the residual, which marks unreachable pixels.
5. **The dye-domain controls:** Halation (through the film's 1-D inverse and forward curves), Saturation, Grain reduction, Grain, Re-shoot on (1-D curve maps).
6. **Printing density** $PD$ from the $c \to PD$ table of the chosen paper and film (a second table for the Re-shoot film, blended).
7. **The printing-density operators** on the edge-aware bases: Contrast mask, Highlights, Shadows, Clarity, Texture.
8. **Paper exposure** $x_k = -PD_k + \mathrm{clear}_k + L_k$, then the light-like controls in order: balance, roll, Exposure, Warmth, Tint, burns, the two pads, Contrast, Pre-flash.
9. **Paper dye amounts** $c_p$ through the paper's three 1-D curves; Fade scales them.
10. **XYZ** from the $c_p \to$ XYZ table: the paper's dyes and base, seen under 5000 K, adapted to D65.
11. **Blacks** (flare) and **Whites** (white mapping, soft shoulder).
12. **Bloom**; then OKLab for Vibrance, Colour density, Saturation rolloff and Hue shifts; then Sharpness.
13. **Encoding** to sRGB or Display P3.

## The tables

| Table | Size | Depends on | Built when |
|---|---|---|---|
| solve: $\mathbf a \to \mathbf c$, residual | 33³ nodes | scanner, film | the source changes |
| $\mathbf c \to PD$ | 33³ nodes | film, paper, lamp | paper or film changes |
| $\mathbf c \to PD$ (Re-shoot film) | 33³ nodes | second film, paper | Re-shoot on changes |
| $c_p \to$ XYZ | 33³ nodes | paper, viewing light, Fade | paper or Fade changes |
| film curves and inverses | 1-D | film (and Re-shoot film) | film changes |
| paper curves | 1-D | paper | paper changes |

The 3-D tables are read with trilinear interpolation.

**The solve table** is the expensive one. Each of its 35,937 nodes is a bounded Levenberg–Marquardt solve of the full broadband equation, with the closed-form Jacobian of §9.3. The Studio's solve holds a dye at its bound when the gradient pushes it out (the active-set step of Chapter 11), and a full table for the X-T5 profile with Gold 200 takes about 0.33 s on one core of our test machine. The Studio builds it on the GPU with WebGPU where it can, otherwise in a pool of workers, otherwise on the main thread in slices so the interface stays responsive. It keeps the table until the scanner or the film changes.

The table is keyed on the analytical estimate, not on raw density. The book found that this tangent keying straightens the function the table must represent and cuts its interpolation error about threefold (§9.7). Where the solve leaves a residual, the densities are outside what the film can produce: the table keeps the bounded answer and flags the pixel as unreachable. The next section explains how the Studio tells apart the reasons a pixel gets flagged.

**The two print tables** cost far less: each node is one pass over the 81 wavelengths, so each rebuilds in about 55 ms on one core, less in the workers or on the GPU. That is the "rebuild" cost class of Chapter 1.

## Blank film, below base, out of gamut

On a real scan, the solve flags pixels for three different reasons, and only one of them is the model's fault. The **Base & gamut** view tells them apart. For each channel it divides the density above the rebate by that channel's noise $\sigma_k$:

$$
z_k = \frac{D_k}{\sigma_k}, \qquad D_k = -\log_{10}\frac{T_k}{T_k^{\text{rebate}}} .
$$

- **Blank film** (blue): some $\lvert z_k\rvert \le 3$. That layer reads at the film's base: it recorded nothing, as in a deep shadow or an underexposed frame. Nothing downstream brings it back; only more exposure on the film would have.
- **Below base** (amber): some $z_k < -3$. A layer reads lighter than unexposed film by more than the noise explains, which the film cannot do. A flat field fixes uneven light across the scan; failing that, check the rebate box.
- **Out of gamut** (red): every $z_k > 3$, with a residual above 0.01 D. Every layer has formed dye, yet no dye amounts fit: the scanner profile or the film stock is wrong.

About seven in eight pixels that are truly at base are flagged unreachable, because noise pushes at least one of their three channels below zero. Blank film prints as a single tone, the film base's, so it shows in the print window as a spike: every blank pixel lands in the same place on the paper's curve, and no control can separate them again.

**The noise.** $\sigma_k$ is measured from the rebate box. Each pixel's departure from the mean of its 9 × 9 neighbourhood is taken, and the median departure times 1.4826 gives $\sigma_k$. A local mean ignores smooth gradients, and unlike differences between neighbours it is not fooled by noise that is correlated over a pixel or two. The Studio measures the noise at two sizes: the view's, and again for the global pass, whose statistics are what the bar and the Print panel report. Without a rebate box, it measures the thin areas of the frame instead. The automatic rebate puts the zero at the frame's lightest pixels, so with it blank film is under-counted.

**On the sample.** The Gold 200 frame is about two stops thinner than a box-speed exposure (Appendix E). With the noise measured at 0.0090, 0.0069 and 0.0122 D, the Studio finds:

- 12.6 % blank film, in the shaded undergrowth on the left and along the bottom;
- 9.7 % below base;
- no pixel out of gamut.

The solve flags 19.3 %. The below-base pixels are 0.03 to 0.07 D lighter than the rebate, too much for noise. The pattern across the frame gives the cause away:

- In the strip of frame nearest the rebate, at the left edge, the deep shadows read at base: 44 to 73 % of it is blank film and at most 13 % is below base.
- Toward the middle and lower middle, the same kind of shadow reads below base, up to a third of the area.

The likeliest cause is light that was stronger in the middle of the scan than at its edge: the missing flat field of Appendix E.

## Why most controls are free

The print from printing density onwards is a fixed function of three numbers per pixel, the paper exposures $x_k$. Everything that moves light only changes those numbers before the table is read:

| Kind of change | Controls |
|---|---|
| an added constant | Exposure, Warmth, Tint, the balance, Roll consistency, Match frame |
| a spatial weight times a constant | Edge burn, Sky burn |
| an affine map per layer | Contrast, the colour pads |
| a 1-D curve per layer | Pre-flash |

None of these touches the table, so every one is a change of a few numbers in the shader and redraws at once. This is what makes a slider for each printer light practical: the physics is computed once; the light is just where you read it.

## The global pass

Spatial controls need neighbourhoods larger than a pixel. The Studio computes them once per change, at low resolution, in a **global pass**:

- the guided-filter coefficients for the large and mid bases (Chapter 6);
- the halation blur of the film's red and green exposures (Chapter 5);
- the bloom blur of the print's bright light, at half the global size (Chapter 8);
- the frame's statistics: large-area densities for the balance, the print window's histograms, and the mean paper exposures for Match frame.

The per-pixel pass then reads these, upsampled, so the per-pixel chain stays per-pixel. Every radius of the global pass is a fraction of the frame's long side, so the preview and the full-size export agree at the same viewing size. Sharpness and Grain reduction are the deliberate exceptions: they work on a few pixels of the rendered image, so judge them at 100 % on the size you export (Chapter 8). Grain is the exception that proves the rule: its noise field is sampled through a mip-map, so its variance follows the area law at every scale (Chapter 5).

## Three resolutions

The Studio works at three sizes:

| Size | Long side | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| global | at most 640 px | the global pass: statistics, guided-filter bases, halation and bloom blurs |
| working | at most 2048 px | the view, snapshots, the ring-around and the working-size export |
| full | the file's own | the full-size export |

The full-size export runs the per-pixel chain in tiles of 1024 px, each with an 8 px border of overlap so that sharpening and the bilateral grain reduction see their neighbours at the seams. The tiles read the same global pass as the view, with their coordinates mapped to the whole frame, so a tiled export matches the preview.

Intermediate images are float32 textures where the GPU can filter them, and half-float otherwise.

## Sources

The Studio opens:

- **TIFF files** (8, 16 or 32-bit; uncompressed, deflate, LZW or PackBits; linear DNG with demosaiced data);
- **PNG and JPEG files**, decoded from sRGB;
- **an R, G, B triplet**: three separate exposures, one per LED, as from a camera scan with sequential lights.

The built-in sample is the real Kodak Gold 200 triplet of Appendix E: a Fujifilm X-T5 under three LEDs, each exposure read through all the photosites of each 6×6 X-Trans block, about 1288 × 860 pixels.

Raw files are not decoded yet. Convert them to linear 16-bit TIFF first, with demosaicing and no tone curve or colour profile.

The **rebate** is found automatically from a bright percentile of the scan, or set by dragging a box over the film's unexposed edge with the Rebate tool of the Source view. The Crop tool sets the frame the same way.

The **flat field** is a capture of the light with no film in the holder: one file, or three for a triplet, one per LED. It must be taken with the same lens, aperture, focus and exposure as the scans, and have the same shape as captured. The Studio reduces it to a coarse grid over the sensor: 96 cells along the long side for *Smooth*, which keeps the illumination and the lens fall-off, or 512 for *Fine*, which also keeps small marks on the light. The grid is smoothed, divided by its mean and floored at 5 %, so the dark surround of a film holder cannot blow up the division. Every pixel of the scan, the rebate included, is then divided by the grid at its place on the sensor, so a rebate measured at the edge holds for the middle of the frame too.

The panel reports the fall-off across the light in density. A flat field is kept for the session only. **Use for all frames** sets it on every frame of the same size, and frames opened after it inherit it. A recipe records its name but not its data. The sample has no flat field: Appendix E's photographer did not capture one.

The **scanner profile** sets the channel responsivities the solve uses:

- X-T5 + RGB LEDs 678/532/472 (fitted, for the sample);
- the book's RGB LEDs at 660/530/450;
- RGB LEDs with custom peaks;
- the book's white LED with a camera;
- a Status M densitometer.

The **film** sets the dyes, the base and the curves.

## Exports

A print exports at the working size or at full size, as:

- PNG at 8 or 16 bits;
- JPEG;
- TIFF at 16 bits, inside a zip.

The 8-bit formats can be encoded for Display P3 as well as sRGB.

Each export can carry its recipe, a JSON file of every control's value plus the source settings: as a second file, or inside the TIFF's zip. **Recipe…** loads one back onto the open frame and reproduces the print. On the hosted page each file is offered through the page and you confirm it before it is saved.

## What is not built yet

The design document describes more than this Studio contains. Missing for now:

- **Capture:** raw decoding, registration of sequential exposures, bracket merging, capture deconvolution (§8.6, §7.6).
- **Clean mode:** IR and learned dust detection, fills in negative density.
- **Local layers:** masks (brush, gradients, density ranges, subject selection), beyond the two burns.
- **The learned light estimator** of §12.7, updated from your own frames.
- **Grain:** noise-spectrum-matched grain reduction, and re-grain at the export size.
- **Output:** ICC soft proofing, output sharpening computed from print size and viewing distance.
- **Restoration mode** for faded and expired negatives, and the scene branch's ADX exports.

Each is a separate section of the design document, with its maths and references.
