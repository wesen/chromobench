# The paper

A negative has no fixed contrast and no fixed white. The paper gives it both. Five controls set the paper and the way the print is seen: **Paper**, **Contrast**, **Pre-flash**, **Whites** and **Blacks**. All five are macros, except Whites beyond paper white. The **print window** shows what they do.

## Paper

The paper is a spectral stock: three layers with their own sensitivities, their own characteristic curves, and their own dyes (§10.4). The Studio carries three, from spektrafilm's profiles:

| Paper | Character |
|---|---|
| Kodak Portra Endura | the default; a portrait paper, moderate contrast, soft highlights |
| Kodak Supra Endura | a general-purpose paper, a little more contrast and saturation |
| Fujifilm Crystal Archive II | steeper curves in red, a punchier colour rendering |

A new paper changes two things, and both tables are rebuilt:

- **What the paper sees of the negative.** Printing density depends on the paper's sensitivities, so the table from dye amounts to printing density is rebuilt.
- **What the paper's dyes look like.** The table from paper dye amounts to XYZ is rebuilt.

Changing paper also changes the aim lights, so the grey card still prints neutral.

The paper's slope is about 2 dye units per unit log H at the grey card, against about 0.5 for a colour negative. The system's contrast is their product, about 1.0 to 1.1 on Portra Endura (§10.4). This is why a negative "looks flat" and the print does not.

> **Try it.** Print on [Supra Endura](st:set?paper=kodak_supra_endura), then on [Crystal Archive](st:set?paper=fujifilm_crystal_archive_typeii), then back on [Portra Endura](st:set?paper=kodak_portra_endura). Each is a rebuild; watch the reds and the shadows.

## The print window

The Studio's histogram is drawn where the print is made: per channel, in paper log exposure $x_k$, after every light-like control. The paper's useful range is shaded on it.

For Portra Endura's red layer that range runs from −0.50 to +0.68 log H relative to the grey point. It goes from the exposure that first raises density 0.04 above the paper's minimum to the one that reaches 90 % of its maximum. That is about 1.2 log H, or 47 points.

The window has a direction that is easy to get backwards. Little paper exposure means little dye and a bright print, so the left end of the axis holds the print's **highlights**. Much exposure means dense dye and a dark print, so the right end holds its **shadows**. Counts left of the band are highlights the paper will burn out to white; counts right of it are shadows it will block up.

> **Try it.** [Open the print window](st:show?view=window), then make the print [a stop brighter](st:set?exposure=1) and [a stop darker](st:set?exposure=-1). The three histograms slide along the axis together. Then try [a softer paper](st:set?exposure=0&contrast=0.75): the histograms narrow.

## Contrast

**Contrast** stretches or squeezes paper exposure about each layer's grey point $g_k$, the exposure that prints the aim grey card:

$$
x_k \leftarrow g_k + \gamma\,(x_k - g_k) .
$$

This is the same as using a paper whose curves are $\gamma$ times steeper, which is what spektrafilm's print gamma factor does to its paper curves. Real colour papers are made in a few fixed contrasts, not on a dial, so Contrast is a macro over materials that are plausible rather than real. It is free: it reshapes the coordinates at which the print table is read.

- **Range:** 0.6 to 1.6, default 1.00.
- **Use:** 0.85–0.95 for contrasty scenes in hard light; 1.1–1.25 for flat light and overcast days.

Far below 1 the print loses its blacks and starts to look like a scan; far above 1 the window narrows and both ends of the scale block up.

> **Try it.** Compare [a soft paper, 0.8](st:set?contrast=0.8) with [a hard one, 1.3](st:set?contrast=1.3), then [return to 1.0](st:set?contrast=1).

## Pre-flash

Printers flashed paper with a short uniform exposure before or after the main one, to hold detail in near-whites. spektrafilm simulates the same flash on its virtual paper. The flash adds a fixed amount of light $H_f$ to every point, in linear exposure:

$$
x_k \leftarrow \log_{10}\!\Big(10^{x_k} + \mathrm{K.PREFLASH}\cdot\tfrac{p}{100}\cdot 10^{\,g_k - 0.6}\Big), \qquad \mathrm{K.PREFLASH} = 0.35 .
$$

The flash is scaled to the exposure 0.6 log H below grey, which sits near the start of the paper's toe. Where the main exposure is large, in midtones and shadows, the flash adds almost nothing. Where it is small, in the brightest highlights, the flash lifts the exposure off the toe, so tones that would print as bare paper get a trace of density and their detail back.

- **Range:** 0 to 100, default 0.
- **Use:** 10 to 40 for a white dress, a cloud or a lamp shade that prints as blank paper. Beyond about 60 the whites turn grey.

Pre-flash is the physical form of highlight recovery. The operator form, **Highlights**, is in Chapter 6.

> **Try it.** [Flash the paper, 30](st:set?preflash=30) and watch the brightest parts of the sign and the wall. Then [overdo it, 90](st:set?preflash=90) to see the whites go grey.

## Whites

A print is seen as a reflection. Its white is the paper's own: unexposed Portra Endura reflects about 81 % of the viewing light. The darkest the paper can go reflects 0.36 %, a visual density of 2.44. A scene's perfect white prints at a density of about 0.16, which leaves room for highlight detail (§10.4).

A display has its own white, so something must map one onto the other. **Whites** sets that mapping:

$$
Y \leftarrow \frac{Y}{Y_{\text{paper}}}\; 2^{\,w/100} .
$$

At 0 the paper's white is the display's white, the natural way to show a print on its own. Negative values dim the print, as a print looks beside a brighter white. Positive values brighten it beyond paper white, and values that would pass the display's white are rolled off with a soft shoulder instead of clipped.

Up to 0 this is a choice of white, which makes it a macro. Above 0 it is a gain with a shoulder, which no print has, so Whites is a hybrid.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** −20 to 0 to show a print as a print; +10 to +30 for a screen image with more sparkle.

## Blacks

The deepest black of a print depends on the room it is seen in. Light reflected off the print's surface and from the room reaches the eye without passing through the dyes. Giorgianni and Madden model this viewing flare as a fraction $f$ of the reproduced white, added at the colour of the viewing light (§10.4, C.9):

$$
(X, Y, Z) \leftarrow (X, Y, Z) + \tfrac{f}{100}\,Y_w \left(\tfrac{x_E}{y_E},\ 1,\ \tfrac{1 - x_E - y_E}{y_E}\right) .
$$

**Blacks** sets $f$ from 0 to `K.FLARE_MAX` = 4 %. The default of 12 on the slider is 0.48 % flare: a glossy print in a dim room. On the textbook's running frame, 1 % flare lifts the black from density 1.96 to 1.75 and moves it towards the light's colour.

Flare is a physical setting of the viewing, so Blacks is a macro.

- **Range:** 0 (gloss, black viewing booth) to 100 (4 %, a matte print in a bright room); default 12.
- **Use:** raise it for a softer, matte look that still keeps the shadows' tonal order.

> **Try it.** Compare [a deep gloss black](st:set?blacks=0) with [a matte print in a bright room](st:set?blacks=75), then [return to the default](st:set?blacks=12).

> **Exercise.** Make the sample look like a matte print on a soft paper: Contrast 0.9, Blacks 60, Whites −10. Which of these three would a printer have controlled, and which belongs to the room?
