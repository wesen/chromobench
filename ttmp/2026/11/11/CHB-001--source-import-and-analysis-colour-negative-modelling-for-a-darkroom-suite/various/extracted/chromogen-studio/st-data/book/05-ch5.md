# Film character

The **Paper and film** section holds the controls that change the materials, or add what the materials do on their own:

- **Re-shoot on** prints the frame as if it had been shot on another film.
- **Halation** adds the red glow a film makes around bright light.
- **Grain** adds dye-cloud grain.
- **Fade** ages the print.
- **Edge burn** and **Sky burn** are the two burns every printer did.

All of them are macros, apart from parts of Grain and the Re-shoot blend, because each one is a setting of something the physical model already contains.

## Re-shoot on

A negative records each layer's exposure through that film's characteristic curve. If the curves are known, the exposures can be recovered and developed again on a different film (§10.5). **Re-shoot on** does this layer by layer:

$$
c^B_k = f^B_k\!\big(f^{A\,-1}_k(c_k)\big),
$$

where $f^A_k$ is the curve of layer $k$ of the film you shot and $f^B_k$ that of the film you choose. The new dye amounts are then printed through film B's own dyes, so film B's colour rendering and its unwanted absorptions come with it: the table from dye amounts to printing density is rebuilt for film B.

Two approximations are built in:

- **Layer exposures carry over one to one.** Film B's red layer is given the exposure that film A's red layer recorded. A full re-exposure would need the scene's spectra, because two films' layers do not see exactly the same wavelengths. What this keeps is the difference the textbook cares most about: the shapes of the curves and the dyes.
- **Noise in the toe is amplified.** The inverse curve multiplies noise by $1/\gamma$, the reciprocal of the local slope (§4.7). Deep shadows of film A, where its curve is flattest, become noisier when re-developed on a steeper film.

**Re-shoot amount** blends the two prints in printing density:

$$
PD = (1 - t)\,PD_A + t\,PD_B .
$$

Only $t = 0$ and $t = 100\,\%$ are prints a darkroom could make, so the amount is a hybrid: a useful operator between its ends. The film choice itself is a macro, and a rebuild.

- **Use:** Ektar 100 for saturation and contrast; Portra 160 for softness; Gold 200 or UltraMax 400 for warmth. Amounts of 30 to 70 % are a gentle push towards a stock.

> **Try it.** [Re-shoot on Ektar 100](st:set?reshoot=kodak_ektar_100&reshootAmount=100), then [halfway](st:set?reshootAmount=50), then [on Portra 160](st:set?reshoot=kodak_portra_160&reshootAmount=100). [Back to the same film](st:set?reshoot=none).

## Halation

Bright light that passes through the emulsion can reflect off the back of the film base and come back up into the emulsion a little further away. The layer nearest the base, the red-sensitive one, receives most of that returning light. An anti-halation layer suppresses it; where it is weak or absent, bright areas grow a soft halo in the red layer (§7.5).

Follow the halo through the chain:

1. More red-layer exposure around a bright area means more cyan dye in the negative there.
2. More cyan in the negative blocks red light at the printer.
3. Less red light means less cyan dye in the print.
4. The halo prints red-orange.

Scatter and halation act on exposure, before the characteristic curve (§7.6, item 1). The Studio therefore applies Halation in the film's own exposure. Each layer's exposure is recovered through the film's inverse curve, $E_k = 10^{f_k^{-1}(c_k)}$. A blurred copy is added to the red and green layers:

$$
E_R \leftarrow E_R + \mathrm{K.HAL\_R}\,\tfrac{h}{100}\,(K * E_R), \qquad E_G \leftarrow E_G + \mathrm{K.HAL\_G}\,\tfrac{h}{100}\,(K * E_G),
$$

with `K.HAL_R` = 0.30 and `K.HAL_G` = 0.08. The new exposure goes forward through the curve to give new dye amounts. The kernel $K$ is a sum of two Gaussians with widths of 0.4 % and 1.8 % of the frame's long side (`K.HAL_RADII`). The blur is computed in the low-resolution global pass, so Halation is a spatial control.

Because the added light is proportional to exposure, the halo only shows around strong highlights: lamps, sunlit edges, specular reflections. A grey wall gains a fraction of its own exposure everywhere and simply prints a little warmer.

- **Range:** 0 to 100, default 0.
- **Use:** 10 to 30 for a hint of the film look; 50 and above imitates film without an anti-halation layer.

> **Try it.** Add [Halation 40](st:set?halation=40) and look at the brightest edges of the sign. Then [turn it up to 90](st:set?halation=90), and [off](st:set?halation=0).

## Grain

A film's grain is made of dye clouds, formed in each layer during development (§7.1, §7.6 item 3). The three layers grain independently, which is why colour grain is speckled in colour rather than in grey. Its variance grows with density: for dye clouds the granularity is roughly proportional to the density formed (Siedentopf, C.7). **Grain** adds a field of that kind to the dye amounts:

$$
c_k \leftarrow c_k + \sigma_0\,\sqrt{c_k + 0.05}\;n_k(x), \qquad \sigma_0 = \mathrm{K.GRAIN\_SIGMA}\cdot\tfrac{g}{100}, \quad \mathrm{K.GRAIN\_SIGMA} = 0.035 .
$$

Here $n_k(x)$ are three independent noise fields with unit variance over the reference aperture, and their correlation length is set by **Grain size**. The 0.05 keeps a trace of grain in the thinnest areas, where the base and the toe still carry a few clouds.

**Grain follows the area law.** Selwyn's law says that granularity $G = A\sigma_A^2$ is constant across apertures (§7.2, C.7), so the standard deviation seen through a pixel falls as the pixel grows: $\sigma^2 \propto G/A$. The Studio samples the noise field through a mip-map, whose levels average areas, so the grain's variance follows the same law at every zoom. At fit-to-screen the grain almost disappears, as it does in a small print. At 100 % it is all there. Judge grain at the size you will print.

The scan already contains the film's own grain. Grain adds more, as if the film were faster; **Grain reduction** (Chapter 8) removes some.

- **Grain size** scales the clouds: 1× is a 400-speed film, 0.5× a fine 100-speed film, 2–3× a pushed or 35 mm-blown-up look.
- **Kind:** at 1× with moderate amounts, Grain is a film's own granularity model, a macro. Large amounts and sizes are invented film, an operator. So Grain and Grain size are hybrids, and both are spatial.
- **Range:** Grain 0 to 100 (default 0); Grain size 0.5× to 3× (default 1×).

> **Try it.** Set [Grain 50 at 1×](st:set?grain=50&grainSize=1) and view at 100 %; then [coarse grain, 2×](st:set?grainSize=2); then zoom out to fit and watch it average away. [Remove it](st:set?grain=0&grainSize=1).

## Fade

Colour prints fade. Their dyes are lost in proportion to their amount, at different rates for different dyes, while the paper base yellows (Gschwind and Frey's model, design document 4.11). **Fade** runs that model forwards on the paper's dyes, to age the print:

$$
c_p \leftarrow F\,c_p, \qquad b_p(\lambda) \leftarrow b_p(\lambda) + \phi\,s(\lambda),
$$

where $F$ is a diagonal matrix whose cyan entry falls fastest, $\phi$ grows with the slider, and $s(\lambda)$ is a yellow stain added to the paper's base density. A faded print therefore drifts towards red, its blacks weaken, and its whites turn cream.

Fade acts on the print's dyes, after the paper's curves, and it rebuilds the table from paper dye amounts to XYZ. It is a macro: a physical model of a real process, applied to a real material.

- **Range:** 0 to 100, default 0.
- **Use:** 10 to 25 for a print that has spent a decade in an album; 60 and beyond for a 1970s drugstore print.

The inverse problem, restoring a faded *negative*, needs the same model in the other direction and is not built yet (design document 4.11).

> **Try it.** [Age the print, Fade 40](st:set?fade=40), then [heavily, 80](st:set?fade=80). [Back to new](st:set?fade=0).

## Edge burn and Sky burn

Printers burned in the edges and corners of almost every print, to hold the eye inside the frame. They burned in skies, which print light because a negative's sky is dense. Both moves are extra printing exposure through a soft mask, so both are printer lights applied locally (§10.2):

$$
x_k \leftarrow x_k + 0.025\,b\,m(r) \quad \text{(Edge burn)}, \qquad x_k \leftarrow x_k + 0.025\,b\,m(y) \quad \text{(Sky burn)} .
$$

$b$ is in points. $m(r)$ rises smoothly from 0 in the middle of the frame to 1 at the corners, and $m(y)$ falls smoothly from 1 at the top edge to 0 part-way down. More exposure means a darker print, so both controls darken.

Because they act on the print table's coordinates, burns cannot halo or change contrast: they are exactly a longer exposure under a card. Both are macros, and both are free.

- **Range:** 0 to 20 points each, default 0.
- **Use:** Edge burn 3 to 8 points (a quarter to two thirds of a stop at the corners); Sky burn 4 to 12 points.

> **Try it.** [Burn the edges, 6 points](st:set?edgeBurn=6), then [burn the sky, 8 points](st:set?skyBurn=8). [Remove both](st:set?edgeBurn=0&skyBurn=0).
