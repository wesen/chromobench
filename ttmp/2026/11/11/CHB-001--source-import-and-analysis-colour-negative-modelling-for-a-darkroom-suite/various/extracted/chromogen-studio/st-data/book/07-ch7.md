# Colour finishing

The print's colour is set by the macros: the lights, the paper and the film. The **Colour** operators then work on top of that print:

- **Saturation** acts on the negative's dyes, before printing.
- **Vibrance**, **Colour density**, **Saturation rolloff** and **Hue shifts** act on the print's colour, after printing, in a perceptual colour space.

The difference between the two places is the most useful thing in this chapter.

## Saturation, in the dyes

A colour negative already enhances saturation chemically. Development inhibitors released in one layer hold back development in its neighbours, so a strongly exposed layer suppresses the others and colours separate more than the dyes alone would give: the interimage effect (§6.1, §6.4). **Saturation** imitates a stronger or weaker interimage effect. It scales each pixel's dye amounts away from their neutral:

$$
\mathbf c' = \mathbf n + \Big(1 + \mathrm{K.DYESAT}\cdot\tfrac{v}{100}\Big)\,(\mathbf c - \mathbf n), \qquad \mathrm{K.DYESAT} = 0.6 ,
$$

where $\mathbf n$ is the dye triple the film forms for a neutral at the same mean exposure: a point on the film's grey ramp. At +100 the departure from neutral grows by 60 %. At −100 it shrinks to 40 %.

The result then goes through the printer, the paper's curves and its dyes like any negative. That has two consequences that a perceptual slider does not have:

- **More saturated colours also get denser in the print.** More dye on the negative means more contrast between layers at the paper, and the paper's dyes absorb more light where colours are strong. Saturated reds deepen instead of glowing.
- **The paper's own limits apply.** A colour cannot leave the paper's gamut, because it is printed by the paper. Its hues bend the way the paper's dyes bend them.

Saturation is an operator: no physical process scales the dyes of a developed negative. The physical route is a film with a stronger interimage effect, which is what Re-shoot on to Ektar 100 partly does (Chapter 5).

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** +10 to +30 for a film-like richness; −20 to −40 for a muted, faded-film look that still prints with real blacks.

> **Try it.** [Saturation +40](st:set?dyeSat=40), then compare with [Vibrance +40 alone](st:set?dyeSat=0&vibrance=40). The first darkens the reds of the sign; the second brightens them.

## OKLab

The operators after the print work in OKLab (Ottosson, 2020). It gives each colour a lightness $L$, a chroma $C$ and a hue angle $h$. It is a perceptual space built from one 3×3 matrix, a cube root and a second 3×3 matrix, which makes it cheap in a shader, and its hues stay steady as chroma changes.

The design document specified CAM16-UCS for these moves. CAM16 models the viewing conditions as well, but it costs more per pixel; the Studio uses OKLab for speed. The print has already been adapted to the display's white (Chapter 1), so the difference matters little for moves of this size.

## Vibrance

**Vibrance** raises chroma most where it is low, and least on skin:

$$
C' = C\,\Big(1 + \mathrm{K.VIBRANCE}\cdot\tfrac{v}{100}\,\big(1 - C/C_{\max}\big)\,\big(1 - 0.7\,w_{\text{skin}}\big)\Big), \qquad \mathrm{K.VIBRANCE} = 0.8 .
$$

Here $C_{\max}$ is a chroma near the edge of the display's gamut, and $w_{\text{skin}}$ is a smooth weight that is 1 at the hue of skin and falls to 0 away from it. Dull colours gain most, colours that are already strong gain little and so do not clip, and faces keep their colour. Negative values mute the print in the same order.

Vibrance keeps lightness, so saturated colours get brighter-looking rather than denser: the opposite of Saturation's character.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** +10 to +30 for landscapes; small negative values for a quiet portrait palette.

> **Try it.** [Vibrance +50](st:set?vibrance=50): the leaves gain more than the already-red sign. [Off](st:set?vibrance=0).

## Colour density

Film and paper make colour by taking light away. A strongly coloured area of a print carries more dye, so it is darker as well as more saturated. Additive colour, on a screen, does the reverse. **Colour density** chooses between those characters:

$$
L' = L - \mathrm{K.COLDENS}\cdot\tfrac{d}{100}\;C, \qquad \mathrm{K.COLDENS} = 0.35 .
$$

Positive values darken saturated colours in proportion to their chroma: richer, heavier, more like a dye-transfer print. Negative values lighten them, a brighter, more digital look. Neutrals ($C = 0$) are untouched, so the tonal scale of greys does not move.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** +15 to +40 with Saturation or Vibrance, to keep strong colours from glowing.

> **Try it.** [Colour density +60](st:set?colourDensity=60), then [−60](st:set?colourDensity=-60). [Off](st:set?colourDensity=0).

## Saturation rolloff

In a print, colour fades towards both ends of the scale. Deep shadows carry nearly the maximum of all three dyes, which leaves little room for colour. Near-whites carry almost no dye at all, which leaves little colour to show. The paper does some of this by itself. **Saturation rolloff** adds more:

$$
C' = C\,\Big(1 - \mathrm{K.ROLLOFF}\cdot\tfrac{r}{100}\;w_{\text{ends}}(L)\Big), \qquad \mathrm{K.ROLLOFF} = 0.85 .
$$

The weight $w_{\text{ends}}(L)$ is 1 near black and near white and 0 through the midtones. At 100 the chroma of the deepest shadows and brightest highlights falls by 85 %. It hides coloured noise in shadows, cleans casts out of specular highlights, and gives the print's ends the look of an optical print.

- **Range:** 0 to 100, default 0.
- **Use:** 20 to 50 for most frames; more to clean up a scan with coloured shadow noise.

> **Try it.** [Saturation rolloff 70](st:set?satRolloff=70) and look at the darkest part of the sign stand. [Off](st:set?satRolloff=0).

## Hue shifts

**Hue shifts** move one family of hues, and its chroma, without touching the others. There are six families, centred at these OKLab hue angles:

| Family | Centre |
|---|---|
| Reds | 25° |
| Skin | 55° |
| Yellows | 100° |
| Greens | 145° |
| Blues | 250° |
| Purples | 320° |

Each family has two values, a hue shift $u$ and a saturation change $s$:

$$
h' = h + \mathrm{K.HUE\_DEG}\cdot\tfrac{u}{100}\,w_i(h), \qquad C' = C\,\Big(1 + \tfrac{s}{100}\,w_i(h)\Big), \qquad \mathrm{K.HUE\_DEG} = 30° .
$$

The weight $w_i(h)$ is a smooth bump around the family's centre, so neighbouring families blend into each other without steps. A positive hue shift turns the family anticlockwise in OKLab: greens towards teal, reds towards orange, blues towards purple. Lightness is not changed.

These are the most computational controls in the Studio. Film and paper move hues together, because three dyes make every colour. Hue shifts can move one family on its own, which is useful and also easy to overdo.

- **Range:** −100 to +100 for each value of each family, default 0.
- **Use:**
  - greens +10 to +30 with saturation −10 to −20 for the teal foliage of many modern film looks;
  - skin saturation −10 to −20 for a softer portrait;
  - blues −10 to −20 to keep skies from turning purple.

> **Try it.** Turn [greens towards teal and quieten them](st:set?hues.greens=30,-20). Then [warm the reds towards orange](st:set?hues.reds=25,0). [Reset both](st:set?hues.greens=0,0&hues.reds=0,0).

> **Exercise.** Make the sample's foliage teal two ways: with Hue shifts on greens, and with Warmth and Tint plus the shadow and highlight pads. Which one leaves the stone wall neutral, and why?
