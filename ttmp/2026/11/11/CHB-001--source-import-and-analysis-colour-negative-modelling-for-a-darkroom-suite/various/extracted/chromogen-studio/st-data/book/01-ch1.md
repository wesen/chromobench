# The print as reference

A colour negative was made to be printed. Its orange mask, its low contrast and its crossed layer sensitivities only make sense once a paper looks at it through a printer's lights (§1.1, §10.1). The Studio therefore takes the print, not the scan, as its reference. Every control is defined by what it does to the print, and the default settings give the print a well-set lab printer would have made.

## The chain

Each pixel passes through seven quantities in order. Each has a unit, and each control acts in exactly one of them.

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | What happens there |
|---|---|---|---|
| linear transmittance | $T_k$ | 0 to 1 | the file, decoded to linear light |
| scanner density | $D_k$ | density | the rebate is subtracted |
| dye amounts | $c_k$ | profile units | the dye solve; Halation, Saturation, Grain, Re-shoot on |
| printing density | $PD_k$ | density | the paper's view; the tone operators |
| paper exposure | $x_k$ | log H | printer lights and every light-like control |
| the print | $c_{p,k}$, XYZ | dye units, CIE XYZ | paper curves, Fade, viewing, Blacks, Whites |
| output | OKLab, RGB | code values | Vibrance, hue moves, Sharpness, Bloom |

Here $k$ runs over the three channels or layers. Scanner channels are R, G, B. Film and paper layers are named by the light they are sensitive to: red, green, blue. Their dyes are cyan, magenta, yellow.

The first steps follow the textbook.

**Scanner density.** The file is decoded to linear transmittance $T$; an 8-bit file is assumed to be sRGB-encoded and is decoded first. Scanner density is taken relative to the rebate, the processed but unexposed film beside the frame (§8.7):

$$
D_k = -\log_{10}\frac{T_k}{T_{k}^{\text{rebate}}} .
$$

**Dye amounts.** The three dye amounts $c$ that reproduce $D$ come from the book's bounded Levenberg–Marquardt solve (§9.4). The solve is tabulated once per scanner and film (§9.7), and its residual is kept as the *unreachable* flag: pixels whose densities no dye triple can produce. The Base & gamut view shows where they are and why: blank film, light below the film's base, or colours outside its dyes (Chapter 10).

**Printing density.** Printing density is the paper's own view of the negative: the dyes' transmittance weighted by the printer lamp (3200 K) and each paper layer's sensitivity (§3.3):

$$
PD_k = -\log_{10} \frac{\sum_\lambda S\,T\,s_k}{\sum_\lambda S\,s_k} .
$$

**Paper exposure.** Finally the paper's log exposure in layer $k$ is (§10.2, C.9)

$$
x_k = -PD_k + \mathrm{clear}_k + L_k ,
$$

where $\mathrm{clear}_k$ is the exposure through an empty gate and $L_k$ the printer light. Everything after that is the paper: its curves turn $x$ into paper dye amounts $c_p$, its dyes reflect light, and that light is seen under a 5000 K viewing source and adapted to the display's white (§10.4).

> **Try it.** Switch between [the negative](st:show?view=negative), [the dyes the solve found](st:show?view=dyes) and [the print](st:show?view=print). The dyes view is the negative without its orange mask.

## Printer points

Printer lights are log-exposure shifts (§10.2). The lab unit for them is the printer point: 0.025 log H (Kodak H-1; C.11). The Studio uses points wherever a control moves light:

- **One stop** of paper exposure is 0.30103 log H, about 12 points.
- **One CC filter step of 0.10 density** in a layer's band is 4 points. A CC10 filter in a darkroom is a 4-point colour move.
- **Paper latitude.** The useful window of Kodak Portra Endura is about 1.2 log H, or 47 points, in its red layer (Chapter 3).

Points are small enough to be honest and large enough to count. A 1-point change is visible on a grey card. A 4-point change is the step of a darkroom ring-around.

## Macros and operators

The most important line in the Studio runs between two families of controls.

**A macro** sets a parameter the physical model already has, such as a printer light, a paper's contrast, a flash of light on the paper, a viewing flare or a different film. With macros alone the Studio can only make a print that a real or plausible film-and-paper chain could make. That is a strong guarantee. You cannot, for example, break a print's tonal order, clip a channel the paper would hold, or move one hue without its neighbours.

**An operator** is a new image operation, outside the model. Examples are an edge-aware contrast mask, a hue shift in a perceptual space, sharpening and bloom. Operators are where the computational freedom lives. They can do what no darkroom could, and they can also make prints that look processed.

**A hybrid** is a macro over part of its range and an operator over the rest. There are three:

- **Whites** is a white mapping up to paper white, and a gain with a soft shoulder beyond it.
- **Grain** at 1× is a film's own granularity, and invented film beyond it.
- **Re-shoot amount** is physical only at 0 and 100 %; between them it blends two prints.

The panel groups the controls this way. **Print**, **Paper and film** and **Roll** are macros. **Tone**, **Colour** and **Detail** are operators, and **Look strength** scales the operators only. A good working habit follows from the grouping: get the print right with the macros, then finish it with the operators.

## Cost classes

Each control also has a cost, which tells you how quickly it responds:

- **free:** the control changes a number in the per-pixel pass. Most macros are of this kind, because they only shift or reshape the coordinates at which the print table is read (Chapter 10). They respond as fast as the display can redraw.
- **rebuild:** the control rebuilds a lookup table. Choosing a paper or a second film, or changing Fade, does this. A rebuild takes some tens of milliseconds, so these controls redraw a moment after you let go (Chapter 11).
- **spatial:** the control needs neighbourhoods: an edge-aware base, a blur or a grain field. These are computed in a low-resolution global pass, then applied at full resolution.

## The order of evaluation

Each control acts in the quantity it physically changes (design document, Section 3). For the controls in this book the order is:

| Quantity | Controls |
|---|---|
| dye amounts | Halation, Saturation, Grain reduction, Grain, Re-shoot on |
| printing density | Re-shoot blend, Contrast mask, Highlights, Shadows, Clarity, Texture |
| paper exposure | Auto colour, Roll consistency, Match frame, Exposure, Warmth, Tint, Edge burn, Sky burn, the two colour pads, Contrast, Pre-flash |
| the print | Paper, Fade, Blacks, Whites |
| output | Bloom, Vibrance, Colour density, Saturation rolloff, Hue shifts, Sharpness |

The order is not arbitrary.

- **Halation** happens in the film, before development, so it belongs in the film's exposure.
- **Contrast masks** were sandwiched with the negative, so they belong in density, where densities add.
- **Printer lights** are exposure, so they belong in log H.
- **Perceptual colour moves** only make sense once there is a colour to perceive.

> **Caveat.** The order is fixed. Two controls in the same quantity commute only approximately, and controls in different quantities do not commute at all. Saturation before printing is not the same as Vibrance after it; Chapter 7 shows the difference.
