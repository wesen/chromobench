# Working a roll

A lab printed a roll, not a frame. The frames of one roll share their film, their processing and their scanner, so the differences between them come from exposure, the light of each scene, and the scene itself. The Studio keeps one set of settings per frame, and the **Roll** controls let frames borrow from each other: **Roll consistency** and **Match frame**. Both are macros, and both are free: they only move printer lights.

## Each frame's own balance

When a frame is opened, its automatic lights come from its own large-area densities (Chapter 2): density fully integrated, colour integrated by Auto colour. Every frame of a roll is therefore printed at a sensible density and roughly neutral without any work.

The weakness is colour failure (§10.3). A frame that is mostly grass, or mostly a red wall, does not average to grey, and integrating it to grey adds the complementary colour to everything else in it. On the textbook's foliage frame the grey card's chroma at full integration is 20.9 (§10.3). Lowering Auto colour helps that frame but weakens the correction of real casts on every other frame.

## Roll consistency

Within a roll, most of the colour differences that matter are shared: the film's own balance, its processing and the scanner. The scene-specific part varies from frame to frame and averages out over many frames. So the mean of a roll's automatic lights is a better estimate of the shared part than any one frame's lights. **Roll consistency** pulls a frame's automatic lights towards that mean:

$$
L \leftarrow (1 - r)\,L_{\text{frame}} + r\,\bar L_{\text{roll}} .
$$

- At 0 the frame keeps its own balance.
- At 100 % every frame is printed with the roll's average lights: one filtration for the whole roll, the way a printer classified a roll once and printed it through (Hunt 16.6).

Exposure, Warmth, Tint and the other light controls act on top. The more frames the roll has, and the more varied their scenes, the better the mean.

- **Range:** 0 to 100 %, default 0.
- **Use:**
  - 50 to 100 % for a roll shot in one light, such as a day outdoors or an evening under the same lamps;
  - 0 to 30 % for a roll that changes light from frame to frame;
  - always worth trying on a frame that is dominated by one colour.

> **Try it.** Load a roll of several frames, choose a foliage-heavy frame, and set [Roll consistency 80 %](st:set?rollConsistency=80). The grey parts of the frame should move towards neutral while the greens stay green. [Back to the frame's own balance](st:set?rollConsistency=0).

## Match frame

Sometimes one frame is already right and the others should look like it: a sequence, a series, a pair on facing pages. **Match frame** takes a reference frame and moves this frame's lights so that its mean paper exposure, the average of $x_k$ over the frame, matches the reference print's:

$$
\Delta L = -m\,\big(\bar x - \bar x_{\text{ref}}\big) .
$$

At 100 % both frames print with the same average colour and density. At lower values the frame moves part of the way.

Matching averages is a strong assumption: it treats the two frames as if they showed the same mix of subjects. It works well for frames of one scene, such as a bracket, a portrait session or a view at different moments. It fails, honestly, for frames of different scenes: a sunset matched to a forest takes on the forest's colour. Use a partial amount, and judge a neutral.

- **Range:** 0 to 100 %, default 0.
- **Use:** 100 % within a sequence of one scene; 30 to 60 % to bring a series closer without flattening it.

> **Try it.** Pick the frame you like best as the reference, open another frame of the same scene, and set [Match frame 100 %](st:set?matchAmount=100). Then [halfway](st:set?matchAmount=50), and [off](st:set?matchAmount=0).

## Snapshots and recipes

A frame's settings are a recipe: a short list of values, one per control, and nothing else. The Studio can keep a snapshot of a recipe and show it beside the current one in a [before/after split](st:show?view=split). Every export carries its recipe in a JSON file next to it, so any print can be remade exactly.

A good order of work for a roll:

1. **Settle the source.** Choose the scanner profile and the film, and check the rebate.
2. **Set the roll's character.** On one representative frame, set the paper and the character (Chapter 5). These are the decisions you will want on every frame.
3. **Balance with the roll in mind.** On each frame, set Exposure, then Roll consistency, then Warmth and Tint, and use the ring-around (Chapter 2) when in doubt.
4. **Finish last.** Add finishing operators per frame, and judge them with Look strength at 0 and 100 %.

> **Caveat.** The design's learned estimator (§12.7), which predicted lights with less than half the error of integrate-to-grey in simulation and was to learn from the frames you balance, is not built yet. Roll consistency is its simplest stand-in: a prior from the roll in place of a prior from you.
