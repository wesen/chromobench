# Tone finishing

The macros of Chapters 2 to 5 set the print. The **Tone** controls finish it: **Contrast mask**, **Highlights**, **Shadows**, **Clarity** and **Texture**. They are operators, outside the physical model, and all of them are spatial: they treat a pixel according to its neighbourhood. They work in printing density, where the darkroom's own tone tools worked, and they share one idea: split the image into a smooth *base* and the *detail* on top of it, then change one without the other.

## Base and detail

Let $\bar Y$ be the mean of the three printing densities at a pixel, a neutral measure of how dense the negative is there. Split it into two parts:

$$
\bar Y = B + (\bar Y - B),
$$

where $B$ is a smooth *base* that follows the large areas of the image and $\bar Y - B$ is the *detail*: texture, edges and small shapes riding on the base. Change the base and you change how light or dark large areas print, without touching their detail. Change the detail and you change local contrast, without moving large areas. This is base/detail tone mapping (Durand and Dorsey, 2002), done here in density.

Every change is applied to all three channels equally:

$$
PD_k \leftarrow PD_k + \Delta(x), \qquad k = R, G, B .
$$

A neutral shift of printing density is a neutral shift of paper exposure, so the tone operators move lightness and leave the print's colour balance alone. The darkroom's contrast masks worked the same way: they were usually made on black-and-white film, so they added neutral density to all three colours.

## The edge-aware base

A plain blur makes a poor base. Near an edge it mixes the two sides, so an operator that changes the base leaves a halo: a dark rim along a bright edge. The darkroom unsharp mask had exactly this rim, and printers used it on purpose to sharpen edges. The Studio uses an **edge-aware** base instead, from the guided filter of He, Sun and Tang (ECCV 2010). In each window of radius $r$, the filter models its output as a linear function of its guide; guided by $\bar Y$ itself:

$$
q = a\,\bar Y + b, \qquad a = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}, \qquad b = (1 - a)\,\mu ,
$$

where $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are the window's mean and variance. In a flat window, $\sigma^2 \ll \varepsilon$, so $a \approx 0$ and the output is the local mean: smooth. Across an edge, $\sigma^2 \gg \varepsilon$, so $a \approx 1$ and the output follows the edge: no halo. The coefficients are averaged over the overlapping windows and applied per pixel. Every step is a box mean, which running sums compute at a cost that does not depend on the radius. The Studio's shaders compute the box means with separable loops instead, whose cost grows with the radius, and keep it small by working at 640 pixels (Chapter 11).

The Studio computes $a$ and $b$ once, at low resolution, in the global pass. It upsamples them and applies them at full resolution (the "fast guided filter"). Two bases are kept:

- the **large** base, radius `K.MASK_RADIUS` = 4 % of the frame's long side, for the contrast mask, highlights and shadows;
- the **mid** base, radius `K.MID_RADIUS` = 0.8 %, for clarity.

Texture uses a plain Gaussian of radius `K.FINE_RADIUS` = 0.09 %, because at that scale there are no large edges to protect. All radii are fractions of the frame, not pixels, so a preview and a full-size export show the same result at the same viewing size.

## Contrast mask

In the darkroom a contrast mask was a weak, unsharp positive printed from the negative and taped to it in register. Where the negative was thin (the print's shadows) the mask added density; where it was dense (the print's highlights) it added little. The sandwich printed with less large-area contrast, while fine detail, too small for the blurred mask to follow, kept all of its contrast. **Contrast mask** does the same on the edge-aware base:

$$
B' = B_{\text{ref}} + \Big(1 - \mathrm{K.MASK}\cdot\tfrac{m}{100}\Big)\,(B - B_{\text{ref}}), \qquad \Delta = B' - B, \qquad \mathrm{K.MASK} = 0.6 .
$$

$B_{\text{ref}}$ is the frame's middle tone. At +100 the base's departures from it shrink to 40 %. Large shadows print lighter, large highlights print darker, and the edges between them stay sharp because the base follows them.

Negative values do the opposite: an *anti-mask* that increases large-area contrast while leaving detail alone. That suits a flat, misty frame.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** +20 to +50 for frames with bright skies and dark foregrounds, the classic case for a mask. −20 to −40 for haze.

> **Try it.** [Contrast mask +40](st:set?mask=40): the dark foliage opens and the bright wall calms down, with no halo along the sign. Compare with [the opposite, −40](st:set?mask=-40). [Off](st:set?mask=0).

## Highlights and Shadows

**Highlights** and **Shadows** change the base only on one side of the frame's middle tone. In printing density the print's highlights are the *dense* end of the negative and its shadows the *thin* end, so:

$$
B' = B - \mathrm{K.ZONE}\,\tfrac{h}{100}\,w_h(B) \quad\text{(Highlights)}, \qquad B' = B + \mathrm{K.ZONE}\,\tfrac{s}{100}\,w_s(B) \quad\text{(Shadows)},
$$

with `K.ZONE` = 0.25 density at ±100. The weights are smooth:

- $w_h(B)$ rises from 0 at the middle tone to 1 in the densest parts of the frame;
- $w_s(B)$ does the same towards the thinnest parts.

Read the signs in print terms:

- **Highlights +** lowers the printing density of bright areas, so the paper gets more exposure there: highlights print darker and recover detail.
- **Highlights −** brightens them.
- **Shadows +** raises the printing density of dark areas, so the paper gets less exposure: shadows open up.
- **Shadows −** deepens them.

The detail $\bar Y - B$ is untouched, so recovered highlights keep their texture and opened shadows keep their contrast.

These are the operator versions of two macros. Pre-flash (Chapter 3) recovers highlights physically, and Blacks (Chapter 3) lifts shadows the way a room does. The operators are local and stronger. The macros are global and always look like a print. Try the macros first.

- **Range:** −100 to +100 each, default 0.
- **Use:** Highlights +20 to +60 for a bright sky; Shadows +20 to +50 for backlit subjects.

> **Try it.** [Recover the highlights, +50](st:set?highlights=50), then [open the shadows, +40](st:set?shadows=40). Compare with the physical route: [reset them](st:set?highlights=0&shadows=0) and try [Pre-flash 40 and Blacks 40](st:set?preflash=40&blacks=40).

## Clarity

**Clarity** raises or lowers mid-scale local contrast, the contrast of shapes a few percent of the frame across: the folds of cloth, the modelling of a face, the stones of a wall.

$$
PD_k \leftarrow PD_k + \mathrm{K.CLARITY}\cdot\tfrac{c}{100}\,\big(\bar Y - B_{\text{mid}}\big), \qquad \mathrm{K.CLARITY} = 0.8 .
$$

Because $B_{\text{mid}}$ is edge-aware, clarity raises contrast inside shapes without outlining them. Negative clarity flattens the mid scale and softens skin and cloth.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** +10 to +30 for landscapes and architecture; −10 to −30 for portraits.

> **Try it.** [Clarity +40](st:set?clarity=40) on the stone wall, then [−40](st:set?clarity=-40). [Off](st:set?clarity=0).

## Texture

**Texture** raises or lowers the finest detail, a fraction of a millimetre on the film:

$$
PD_k \leftarrow PD_k + \mathrm{K.TEXTURE}\cdot\tfrac{t}{100}\;\mathrm{core}\big(\bar Y - G * \bar Y\big), \qquad \mathrm{K.TEXTURE} = 1.2 .
$$

The **coring** function passes large differences and shrinks small ones towards zero. Grain lives in the small differences, so Texture sharpens leaves, hair and fabric without turning up the grain as much. Coring is a soft threshold, not a grain model: at high settings grain still rises.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** +10 to +30 for foliage and fabric; negative values soften fine detail without blurring edges.

> **Try it.** [Texture +50](st:set?texture=50) and view the leaves at 100 %. [Off](st:set?texture=0).

## Look strength

**Look strength** $\lambda$ scales every finishing operator at once: each operator's value is replaced by $\lambda v$. It runs from 0 to 150 %, and the default is 100 %. At 0 the print is exactly the physical print the macros made. At 150 % every operator is half as strong again.

The macros are not scaled, so Look strength can never change a print's exposure, balance, paper or film. That makes it the safest way to judge whether the finishing is doing too much: drop it to 0, look, and bring it back.

> **Try it.** Set up a strong finish, [mask, clarity and saturation](st:set?mask=40&clarity=30&dyeSat=25), then [turn Look strength to 0](st:set?look=0) and [back to 100](st:set?look=100).

> **Caveat.** The tone operators change printing density, so they also change what the paper sees. A strong contrast mask moves tones back into the paper's window, as a real mask did; the [print window](st:show?view=window) shows the histograms narrowing.
