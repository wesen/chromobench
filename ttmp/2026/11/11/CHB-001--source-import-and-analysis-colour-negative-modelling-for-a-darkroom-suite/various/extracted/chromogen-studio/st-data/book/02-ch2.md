# Light and colour of the print

Four controls set the printer lights: **Exposure**, **Warmth**, **Tint** and **Auto colour**. Together they do what the lab printer's density and colour keys did. They are all macros and all free: each adds to the three paper exposures $x_k$ before the print table is read.

## Where the lights come from

A print starts from a balance. The Studio integrates to grey, the method that made automatic printing of amateur negatives work (Evans; Hunt 16.7; §10.3).

1. Measure the frame's large-area transmission density in each paper band. It is the density of the mean transmittance, so thin areas of the negative weigh more:
   $$
   \mathrm{LATD}_k = -\log_{10}\big\langle 10^{-PD_k} \big\rangle_x .
   $$
2. Compare it with an aim $A_k$, the printing density of the film's own grey card: the negative of a grey exposed at log H = 0 in every layer. The departures are $\Delta_k = \mathrm{LATD}_k - A_k$, and their mean is $\bar\Delta$.
3. Set the lights:
   $$
   L_k = a_k + \bar\Delta + \alpha\,(\Delta_k - \bar\Delta) ,
   $$
   where $a_k$ are the lights that print the aim grey card neutral at a reflectance of 0.184 (the grey-patch balance of B.12).

The term $\bar\Delta$ is the density correction. It is always applied, so a frame two stops over prints at the same density as a normal one. The term $\alpha(\Delta_k - \bar\Delta)$ is the colour correction, and $\alpha$ is the **Auto colour** control.

Exposure, Warmth and Tint are then moves away from that balance. At their defaults the print is the balanced print.

## Exposure

**Exposure** makes the print brighter or darker. In darkroom terms it is the printing time. Its unit is stops of paper exposure, and +1 means one stop *brighter*, which is one stop *less* printing exposure:

$$
x_k \leftarrow x_k - 0.30103\,e \qquad (e \text{ in stops}).
$$

The panel shows the same move in points: 12 points per stop, with the sign of the light. A positive Exposure removes light from all three layers equally, so it changes density and not colour.

- **Range:** −3 to +3 stops, default 0.
- **Use:** most frames need less than half a stop, because integration already balances density. Larger moves are for frames where the average misleads, such as a subject against a large bright sky or snow.

> **Try it.** [Half a stop brighter](st:set?exposure=0.5), then [half a stop darker](st:set?exposure=-0.5). Watch the [print window](st:show?view=window): the three histograms move together along the paper's exposure axis.

## Warmth

**Warmth** makes the print warmer or cooler, along the direction the light of the scene would have moved it. The direction comes from the model, not from a guess:

1. Expose the film's grey card under blackbody light at a range of colour temperatures.
2. Compute the grey card's printing density at each.
3. Differentiate with respect to the mired value $M = 10^6/T$, and remove the mean of the three layers so that warmth does not change density.

Warmth then adds the opposite of the correction a printer would have made:

$$
\Delta x = -\frac{\partial PD_{\text{grey}}}{\partial M}\,\big(\mathrm{K.WARM\_MIRED}\cdot w\big), \qquad \mathrm{K.WARM\_MIRED} = 1.3 .
$$

So ±100 is ±130 mired. On Portra 400 or Gold 200, moving from 5500 K to 3200 K (131 mired) changes the grey card's printing density by about +0.13 log H in red and −0.13 in blue, relative to the mean. In points, Warmth +100 removes about 5 points of red light and adds about 5 points of blue: less cyan and more yellow in the print. The green layer barely moves, which is why warmth in a negative is not the same as "add yellow".

The panel shows the mired equivalent beside the value.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** ±10 to ±30 for taste. The full range is the difference between daylight and tungsten, and integration already removes most of a real light's cast (Auto colour).

> **Try it.** [Warm the print to evening light](st:set?warmth=40), then [cool it to open shade](st:set?warmth=-40), then [go back](st:set?warmth=0). Watch the stone wall: it follows the daylight locus, not a straight line towards yellow.

## Tint

**Tint** moves the print towards magenta (+) or green (−). It adds green light against red and blue, keeping the mean of the three layers constant:

$$
\Delta x = 0.025\cdot\mathrm{K.TINT\_PT}\cdot\tfrac{t}{100}\,\big(-\tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ -\tfrac13\big), \qquad \mathrm{K.TINT\_PT} = 10 .
$$

At +100 the green layer receives 10 points more than red and blue, which is two and a half CC10 steps of magenta in the print.

- **Range:** −100 to +100, default 0.
- **Use:** fluorescent and mixed light often need −10 to −30. Skin under foliage often needs a little + to take out green.

Warmth and Tint together span the two colour dimensions of the lights; Exposure is the third. Any printer-light correction can be written as a combination of the three.

## Auto colour

**Auto colour** is $\alpha$ in the integration formula, in per cent:

- At **100 %** the print's average is made neutral. That removes the colour of the scene light completely, and also the colour of a scene that genuinely does not average to grey.
- At **0 %** the colour comes from the aim alone. The film's balance for its rated light is kept, and so is any cast.

The default of 75 % is the book's partial correction (Hunt 16.10). On a frame that is 70 % foliage, full integration leaves the grey card with a chroma $C^*_{ab}$ of 20.9; 75 % lowers it to 15.7 and 50 % to 10.4 (§10.3). The cost is a weaker correction of the scene light. This trade-off is *colour failure*, and no single setting removes it. Chapter 9 shows how a roll helps.

- **Range:** 0 to 100 %, default 75 %.
- **Use:** lower it for frames dominated by one colour (foliage, a red wall, a sunset you want to keep). Raise it for frames under odd light whose colour you want gone.

> **Try it.** Set [Auto colour to 100 %](st:set?autoColour=100), then [to 0 %](st:set?autoColour=0), then [back to 75 %](st:set?autoColour=75). On the sample the change is mostly in the greens of the leaves.

## The ring-around

The darkroom ring-around shows the current print surrounded by six variations, one step away in each primary and secondary direction, plus lighter and darker versions. Opposite tiles are opposite moves, so a cast is fixed by choosing the tile that looks right: a print that is too magenta is fixed by the tile that adds green. Each tile is the same print table read at shifted coordinates, so the tiles are exact and cost no more than the main view.

The step is set in points:

| Step | Darkroom equivalent |
|---|---|
| 2 pt | CC05 |
| 4 pt | CC10 |
| 8 pt | CC20 |

> **Try it.** [Open the ring-around](st:show?view=ring). Choose a step, click the tile that looks right, then halve the step and repeat. Two rounds usually settle a frame.
