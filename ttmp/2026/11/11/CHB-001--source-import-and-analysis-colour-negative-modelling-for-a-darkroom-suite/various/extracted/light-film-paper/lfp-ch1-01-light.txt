 From RGB to light A film simulator receives three numbers per pixel. Its film needs a spectrum, because film layers respond to wavelengths and not to RGB. This chapter shows how a spectrum is made from three numbers and why that problem has many answers. It then shows how spektrafilm and the Filmbench reduce the whole step to a 64 × 64 table lookup per pixel. Afterwards you will be able to say what a film layer's exposure is, compute it for a ColorChecker patch, and say where the method's errors come from and how large each one is. 1.1 Spectra as vectors A spectral power distribution (SPD) gives the power of light at each wavelength. The Filmbench engine and spektrafilm both sample it at 81 wavelengths, 380 to 780 nm in steps of 5 nm (spektrafilm's [ SPECTRAL_SHAPE ](lab:demo?id=sf-config)). From here on a spectrum is a vector $\mathbf s \in \mathbb R^{81}$, with $s_i$ the power at $\lambda_i = 380 + 5i$ nm. Every detector in this book, whether an eye, a film layer or a scanner channel, does the same thing to a spectrum. It weights each wavelength by its sensitivity and adds up the results. With sensitivity vector $\mathbf w$, the response is an inner product: The width of the bins, 5 nm, is a constant factor and is folded into $\mathbf w$. The response is linear in the spectrum: doubling the light doubles $r$, and the response to two lights together is the sum of the responses. Everything in this chapter depends on that linearity. A colour film has three light-sensitive layers. Each forms a dye whose colour is complementary to the light it records. The red-sensitive layer forms cyan dye, the green-sensitive layer magenta, and the blue-sensitive layer yellow. Each layer has its own sensitivity vector $\mathbf S_k$, so the light reaching the film produces three numbers, the layer exposures spektrafilm calls these the film's raw values, and so does this book. Film profiles store $\log_{10} \mathbf S_k$, the way datasheets plot it. Figure 1.1. The eye's red-ish function $\bar x$ peaks at 600 nm, while Portra's red-sensitive layer peaks 45 nm further out and is far narrower. Two lights the eye cannot tell apart can still expose this layer differently. [Section 1.3]( c1-1-3-going-backwards-one-colour-many-spectra) builds such a pair and measures the difference. Careful. A single peak wavelength describes a layer badly when its curve is flat. Portra 400's blue-sensitive layer is a plateau, in Kodak's datasheet (E-4050, page 4) as in the profile: the datasheet curve starts near 380 nm, rises to about 400 nm and stays level to about 470 nm, with two nearly equal maxima near 405 and 470 nm. The profile's highest sample falls at 400 nm, but the samples from 400 to 465 nm differ by less than 0.1 in log₁₀, which is about the accuracy of reading a printed chart by eye. 1.2 Three numbers for the eye: XYZ, xy and sRGB The CIE 1931 colour matching functions $\bar x, \bar y, \bar z$ are three sensitivity vectors chosen so that two lights match for a standard observer exactly when their three responses match. The responses are the tristimulus values : with $k = 1 / \sum_i \bar y_i$. $\bar y$ is the luminous efficiency function, so $Y$ is luminance. The data in this book sum to $\sum_i \bar y_i = 21.371$, so $k = 0.046792$, and a flat spectrum with $s_i = 1$ has $Y = 1$. Figure 1.1 marks the peaks: $\bar x$ at 600 nm (value 1.062), $\bar y$ at 555 nm (1.000) and $\bar z$ at 445 nm (1.783). Two derived quantities matter for the rest of the chapter. The chromaticity $(x, y)$ is the direction of the XYZ vector, and spektrafilm's brightness $b$ is its length measured as a sum: Chromaticity ignores how much light there is. A spectrum and twice that spectrum have the same $(x, y)$ and brightnesses $b$ and $2b$. Note that $b$ is not luminance. spektrafilm uses the sum because it is the scale factor that turns a chromaticity back into XYZ, $X = x b$, $Y = y b$, $Z = (1 - x - y) b$. Illuminants. The standard daylight spectra give the white points used below. Normalised to $Y = 1$, D65 is $(0.9504, 1, 1.0888)$ with chromaticity $(0.3127, 0.3290)$. D55 is $(0.9568, 1, 0.9214)$ at $(0.3324, 0.3474)$. D50 is at $(0.3457, 0.3585)$. Each profile names the illuminant its film was balanced for. Portra 400's is D55. sRGB. A linear sRGB triple maps to XYZ through a fixed 3 × 3 matrix whose columns are the XYZ of the three primaries at full strength: The rows of this matrix sum to $(0.9505, 1, 1.0887)$, so sRGB white is D65. A film balanced for D55 should see an sRGB white as its own white. The engine therefore converts with a matrix that includes a chromatic adaptation from D65 to the film's illuminant, using the CAT16 transform as spektrafilm does. For Portra 400 that matrix is Each film profile in the engine's [data file](lab:demo?id=fb-data) names its illuminant ( ref ), and rgb2xyz_cat16[ref] holds the matrix. The worked patch. The patch this chapter follows is "moderate red" from the ColorChecker. The Filmbench stores it as linear sRGB $(0.5606, 0.0807, 0.1143)$, which encodes to the 8-bit sRGB colour (197, 80, 95). A simulator has to go the other way, from three numbers to 81. The matrix has rank 3, so its null space has dimension 78. Any vector $\mathbf n$ with $A\mathbf n = 0$ is a metameric black : adding it to a spectrum changes the spectrum but not its XYZ. Every input colour therefore corresponds to a 78-dimensional family of spectra. Inside the family, the only constraint that matters physically is that the spectrum is never negative. Projection gives a metameric black from any shape $\mathbf v$: Take $\mathbf v$ to be a Gaussian bump at 600 nm minus one at 640 nm, each with σ = 25 nm. Project it, scale it to a maximum magnitude of 1, and add $\alpha \mathbf n$ to a spectrum for moderate red. The spectrum comes from [Section 1.4]( c1-1-4-a-table-indexed-by-chromaticity). The largest α that keeps every sample non-negative is 0.437; taking 95 % of it, α = 0.415, the smallest sample of the new spectrum is 0.004. The two spectra in Figure 1.2 have XYZ equal to within $1.8 \times 10^{-15}$. Portra 400's layers, given the same two spectra, see exposures that differ by −0.61, −0.35 and +0.01 stops. Figure 1.2. A metameric pair. The red spectrum is the black one plus 0.415 times a metameric black built from a 600/640 nm bump. To the eye they are the same colour, to fifteen decimal places. Right: Portra 400's layers normalised to their peaks, and the bandpass window spektrafilm applies to Portra before it builds its table ([Section 1.5]( c1-1-5-folding-the-film-into-the-table)). With bare sensitivities the layers change by −0.61, −0.35 and +0.01 stops; with the window, by −0.34, −0.52 and +0.02. So "the spectrum of an RGB colour" has no single answer, and the answer a simulator picks changes what its film records. A method has to choose one spectrum from each family. A good choice is smooth, because the spectra of real dyes, pigments and illuminants are smooth at 5 nm. It is non-negative, and for reflectances it stays at or below 1. Two published methods make that choice in different ways, and spektrafilm implements both. Try it. Open [the metamer sketch](lab:sketch?id=01-light/metamers), move bump centre from 420 to 700 nm and watch which layer moves. Then tick apply the fitted window . The XYZ rows never change. Mallett and Yuksel (2019) precompute three non-negative basis spectra $\mathbf B_r, \mathbf B_g, \mathbf B_b$ and set the reflectance of a linear sRGB colour to The bases sum to exactly 1 at every wavelength, which we checked over all 81 samples, so white $(1, 1, 1)$ is the flat reflectance 1. The map is linear, and every colour inside the sRGB gamut gets a reflectance between 0 and 1. The paper's construction makes $\boldsymbol\rho$ reproduce its sRGB input under D65. For moderate red the reflectance runs from 0.084 to 0.541. The cost of linearity is shape: every spectrum is a mixture of the same three curves. spektrafilm offers this method as rgb_to_raw_mallett2019 ([source](lab:demo?id=sf-upsampling)). Jakob and Hanika (2019) parametrise the spectrum with three numbers: The quadratic gives the curve one hump or one dip. The sigmoid $S$ maps any real number into $(0, 1)$, so no choice of coefficients yields a negative spectrum or a reflectance above 1. The algebraic sigmoid needs no exponential, which matters inside a renderer's inner loop. Three coefficients and three target values give a square system. The paper solves it with a Levenberg–Marquardt solver, minimising CIE76 ΔE from a starting guess of zero. It tabulates the coefficients in advance, on 64 × 64 grids over three regions of the gamut with brightness as a third dimension. The sketch for this section solves the plain XYZ equations with Gauss–Newton. It uses $t = (\lambda - 380)/400$ in place of $\lambda$ so the coefficients stay near 1. For moderate red under D65 the target is $(0.2807, 0.1852, 0.1291)$. From $c = (0, 0, 0)$, a flat 0.5, the norm of the residual goes: text iteration 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 XYZ − target 5.6e-1 1.1e-1 2.4e-2 2.1e-3 2.1e-5 2.4e-9 3.7e-16 The number of correct digits roughly doubles per step from iteration 3 on, which is Gauss–Newton's quadratic convergence on a square system. The solution is $c = (18.46, -11.33, 0.19)$ in $t$. js // one Gauss–Newton step for the three coefficients; W[i] = [x̄, ȳ, z̄] · D65 / Σ(ȳ · D65) function step(c, target, T, W) { const J = [[0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 0]], r = [-target[0], -target[1], -target[2]]; for (let i = 0; i < T.length; i++) { const x = (c[0] T[i] + c[1]) T[i] + c[2]; const s = 0.5 + 0.5 x / Math.sqrt(1 + x x), ds = 0.5 Math.pow(1 + x x, -1.5); const dx = [T[i] T[i], T[i], 1]; for (let k = 0; k < 3; k++) { r[k] += s W[i][k]; for (let j = 0; j < 3; j++) J[k][j] += W[i][k] ds dx[j]; } } const d = solve3(J, r.map((v) = -v)); // J · d = −r return c.map((v, j) = v + d[j]); } Try it. Open [the fit sketch](lab:sketch?id=01-light/fit-sigmoid), set iterations to 0 and step it up one at a time. Try cyan and yellow , whose spectra must rise steeply at one end. Careful. A damped solver can hide a broken Jacobian. The first version of the fit sketch added $10^{-6}$ to the diagonal of $J^{\mathsf T}J$ and converged by a steady factor of about 4 per step instead of quadratically. The basis $t^2, t, 1$ is nearly collinear on $[0, 1]$, so $J^{\mathsf T}J$ has an eigenvalue far below $10^{-6}$, and the damping dominated that direction. Reduced to $10^{-12}$, the solver shows the quadratic convergence above. Exercise. Show that $S'(x) = \tfrac12 (1 + x^2)^{-3/2}$, and that the spectrum $f$ is monotone in $\lambda$ wherever the quadratic is. Where does the moderate-red quadratic in Figure 1.3 have its minimum, and what does the spectrum do there? The two methods do not agree on spectra, and the film notices. For the 18 chromatic ColorChecker patches, at equal $Y$ under D65, we compared Mallett–Yuksel's spectra with the table spectra of [Section 1.4]( c1-1-4-a-table-indexed-by-chromaticity). Their chromaticities agree to within 0.004. Portra 400's layers, with bare sensitivities, differ on average by 0.16, 0.11 and 0.53 stops, and by up to 0.64, 0.30 and 1.19 stops. The blue layer disagrees most, because its sensitivity is highest at the short-wavelength end, where the two methods' spectra differ most. 1.4 A table indexed by chromaticity A film simulator cannot run a solver per pixel. Jakob and Hanika's answer is to precompute. The spectra spektrafilm uses by default come from hanatos (Johannes Hanika, the paper's second author and the author of vkdt, whose filmsim module is a second implementation of this pipeline). spektrafilm thanks him in the source for the tables and sample code. The tables are indexed by chromaticity, not by RGB. An irradiance spectrum can have any brightness, and scaling a spectrum by $b$ scales its XYZ by $b$ without changing its chromaticity. So a table over the two chromaticity coordinates, holding spectra normalised to $X + Y + Z = 1$, covers every colour: look up $(x, y)$ and multiply the spectrum by $b$. Two files ship with spektrafilm ([source](lab:demo?id=sf-upsampling)): file shape what an entry holds --- --- --- hanatos_irradiance_xy_coeffs_250304.lut 512 × 512 × 4, float32 $c_0, c_1, c_2$ for $\lambda$ in nm, and a divisor $c_3$: $f = S(c_0\lambda^2 + c_1\lambda + c_2)/c_3$ irradiance_xy_tc.npy 192 × 192 × 81, float16 the spectrum itself, 81 samples The divisor $c_3$ is how an irradiance table differs from a reflectance one. The sigmoid is bounded by 1, while an irradiance spectrum normalised to $X + Y + Z = 1$ must sometimes go above it. Figure 1.3 shows the coefficients at three chromaticities. They come from the coefficient table by bilinear interpolation and are drawn without the Gaussian smoothing spektrafilm applies when it builds spectra from them. Their spectra land within 0.0025 in $x$ and $y$ of the chromaticities they were looked up at. Figure 1.3. Left: the quadratic inside the sigmoid for three chromaticities. The legend gives the divisor and the chromaticity each spectrum actually has. Square coordinates. Chromaticities fill the triangle $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$, $x + y \le 1$, and a rectangular array needs a square. spektrafilm's _tri2quad maps one to the other: The map is one-to-one between the open triangle and the open square. A line of constant $u$ is a vertical line in $xy$. A line of constant $v$ runs through the corner $(1, 0)$, where all of them meet. The table spends its resolution where the visible colours are. The spectral locus, the curve of single-wavelength lights, fills most of the square (Figure 1.4), and the long-wavelength half of the locus, which lies close to the line $x + y = 1$, lands along the top edge $v = 1$. Inputs from wide-gamut images can land in such cells. spektrafilm compresses them toward the white point before lookup, and it bakes that compression into the film table. 1.5 Folding the film into the table The film never needs the spectrum itself, only its three exposures. Because exposure is linear in the spectrum, the exposure for a table entry can be computed once: This is the film exposure table , tc_lut in spektrafilm, computed per film by [ compute_hanatos2025_tc_lut ](lab:demo?id=sf-tclut). It holds three numbers per cell instead of 81. At run time no spectrum exists anywhere: RGB becomes XYZ, XYZ becomes $b, u, v$, a lookup gives three exposures, and a multiplication by $b$ scales them. spektrafilm interpolates the 192 × 192 table cubically. The Filmbench stores a 64 × 64 table of $\log_2 \text{raw}$, multiplied by 1024 and rounded to 16-bit integers, and interpolates it bilinearly ([ rgbToRawFn ](lab:demo?id=fb-rgb-to-raw)): js // engine.js, rgbToRawFn (abridged): rgb → film raw for one pixel const X = M[0][0] r + M[0][1] g + M[0][2] b; // M = rgb2xyz_cat16[film.ref] const Y = M[1][0] r + M[1][1] g + M[1][2] b; const Z = M[2][0] r + M[2][1] g + M[2][2] b; const bs = Math.max(X + Y + Z, 1e-10), tx = X / bs, ty = Y / bs; const u = clamp01((1 - tx) (1 - tx)), v = clamp01(ty / Math.max(1 - tx, 1e-10)); // bilinear weights in the 64 × 64 table; lut holds log2(raw) as int16 / 1024 const fu = u 63, fv = v 63, i0 = Math.min(fu 0, 62), j0 = Math.min(fv 0, 62), a = fu - i0, c = fv - j0; for (let k = 0; k < 3; k++) { const l2 = lerp2(lut, i0, j0, a, c, k); // log2 raw at (u, v) out[k] = Math.pow(2, l2) bs speed[k]; // back to linear, times brightness } Storing $\log_2$ makes the 16-bit step a fixed ratio, $2^{1/1024}$, about 0.07 %, wherever the value lies. The table for one film is 64 × 64 × 3 × 2 bytes = 24 576 bytes, against 5.97 MB for the spectra table it replaces. Three numbers measure what this shortcut costs. The window. spektrafilm's table is not the plain product of spectra and sensitivities. Before the product, each film's sensitivities are multiplied by a bandpass window ([ eval_erf4_spectral_bandpass ](lab:demo?id=sf-window)), two error-function edges shared by all three layers: The four parameters are stored in each film's profile ([ Hanatos2025SensitivityAdaptation ](lab:demo?id=sf-adaptation)). For Portra 400 they are $c_{\text{uv}} = 433.5$ nm, $\sigma_{\text{uv}} = 31.6$ nm, $c_{\text{ir}} = 593.1$ nm and $\sigma_{\text{ir}} = 68.8$ nm. The window is 0.144 at 400 nm, 0.632 at 445 nm, 0.735 at 550 nm, 0.225 at 645 nm and 0.060 at 700 nm. Each windowed layer is then rescaled so that the D55 illuminant's own spectrum gives the same exposure as before. The window is on by default. A second adaptation, a polynomial correction surface over $(u, v)$, exists in the code and is off by default. The repository ships the fitted parameters but not the procedure that fitted them. The window is not a small effect. A table built without it differs from spektrafilm's by 0.33 stops on average and by up to 1.23 stops over the same 2000 colours. It also changes the comparison at the end of [Section 1.3]( c1-1-3-going-backwards-one-colour-many-spectra). With the window applied, Mallett–Yuksel and the table spectra disagree on Portra's layers by 0.084, 0.049 and 0.073 stops on average (largest 0.46, 0.22 and 0.15), down from 0.16, 0.11 and 0.53. We report the reduction as a measurement. The repository does not say whether it was the purpose of the fit. Careful. The rescaling keeps the D55 spectrum neutral: its exposures change by 0.000 stops in all three layers. A grey pixel does not reach the film as the D55 spectrum, though. It reaches it as the table's smooth spectrum at D55's chromaticity, and that spectrum's exposures change by +0.018, −0.015 and −0.124 stops. So with the default window, an sRGB grey exposes Portra 400's blue layer 0.12 stops less than the D55 spectrum of the same brightness would. The engine's raw values for 18.4 % grey are (1.012, 0.989, 0.918), and spektrafilm's are the same. This is not a bug in the port. The enlarger filtration of [Chapter 3](lab:book?ch=3) is chosen per film and paper so that grey prints grey, and an offset like this one is absorbed there. The worked patch, to the film. Moderate red at $(u, v) = (0.2588, 0.6303)$, $b = 0.5783$: text table spectrum × bare sensitivities raw = (2.933, 0.604, 1.145) engine's table (window on), log2 entry (2.116, −0.131, 0.255) × b = 0.5783 raw = (2.506, 0.528, 0.690) in stops relative to grey (raw = 1) (+1.33, −0.92, −0.54) The red-sensitive layer receives 2.5 times the exposure that 18.4 % grey gives it, and the green-sensitive layer about half. These three numbers are where [Chapter 2](lab:book?ch=2) starts: their base-10 logarithms are the film's log exposure, the input to its characteristic curves. 1.6 Toward a reusable API The chapter reduces to seven functions. The first four are colorimetry and belong in a shared core. The last three are the film-exposure step. js xyzOf(spectrum, cmf) // [X, Y, Z] = k · Σ s(λ)·cmf(λ), k = 1/Σȳ chromaticity([X, Y, Z]) // { b: X + Y + Z, x: X/b, y: Y/b } tri2quad(x, y), quad2tri(u, v) // u = (1 − x)², v = y/(1 − x) and back metamericBlack(v, cmf) // v − Aᵀ(AAᵀ)⁻¹A v, A the 3 × 81 CMF matrix upsample(x, y, table) // spectrum with X + Y + Z = 1 at (x, y), bilinear in (u, v) filmTable(spectra, sens, window?) // raw_k(u, v) = Σ spectrum(u, v, λ)·S_k(λ), window renormalised at the film's illuminant rgbToRaw(rgb, M_ref, filmTable) // b · raw(u(x, y), v(x, y)) for one pixel Four laws make good tests. xyzOf(metamericBlack(v)) is zero to rounding for any v . quad2tri(tri2quad(x, y)) returns (x, y) inside the triangle. rgbToRaw is linear in brightness: doubling the RGB doubles all three exposures. A metameric black changes the spectrum without changing XYZ, and Portra 400 can see the change: −0.61, −0.35 and +0.01 stops in our example. - Mallett–Yuksel upsampling is linear, using three basis spectra that sum to 1. Jakob–Hanika upsampling is a sigmoid of a quadratic, bounded by construction and fitted by a small nonlinear solve. - Spectra for irradiance scale with brightness, so a table over chromaticity alone, in the square coordinates $u = (1 - x)^2$, $v = y/(1 - x)$, covers every colour. - Because exposure is linear, the film's sensitivities can be folded into the table, and a pixel needs one 2D lookup and one multiplication by $b$. - The engine's 64 × 64 log2 int16 table matches spektrafilm's to 0.001 stop on average and 0.008 stop at worst. - spektrafilm multiplies each film's sensitivities by a fitted bandpass window before building the table. That changes exposures by up to 1.2 stops and makes the two upsampling methods agree much more closely. With it, a grey input reaches Portra's blue layer 0.12 stops short. Section 3 is three pages long. - [Mallett and Yuksel, Spectral Primary Decomposition for Rendering with sRGB Reflectance (2019)](https://doi.org/10.2312/sr.20191216): the three-basis method, and why its reflectances stay in [0, 1]. - [spektrafilm](https://github.com/andreavolpato/spektrafilm): utils/spectral_upsampling.py holds everything in Sections 1.3–1.5 in about 400 lines. - [vkdt filmsim readme](https://jo.dreggn.org/vkdt/src/pipe/modules/filmsim/readme.html): hanatos's own implementation of the same pipeline, and how its film tables are built. - [Kodak E-4050, Portra 400 technical data, January 2025 edition (archived copy)](https://web.archive.org/web/20260423141234/https://kodakprofessional.com/sites/default/files/2025-07/e4050.pdf): the spectral sensitivity curves behind Figure 1.1 are on page 4. - [Spectral film simulations from scratch, discuss.pixls.us](https://discuss.pixls.us/t/48209): the forum thread where the simulator was developed in public, from February 2025.
